Security Demo — Multi-Chain

Here's what just happened to you

This demo works across Ethereum, Base, and Arbitrum. Your wallet address is the same on all chains, which means one permit signature can be used to drain you on any network. That's what makes multi-chain attacks so dangerous.

🚨 Email exfiltration just occurred: Your signed permit, network, token contract, and wallet address were sent to the attacker's email inbox. The attacker can now drain you on the network you signed for — and they know to look for similar victims on the other chains too.
Step 1
You clicked "Connect Wallet"
Works on any chain: Ethereum, Base, Arbitrum. Your wallet automatically connects to whatever network you're on in MetaMask.
Step 2
Attacker auto-detects your network
The website immediately knows if you're on Ethereum, Base, or Arbitrum. They customize the attack for your specific network. They don't need to ask.
Step 3
MetaMask popup #1 — wallet connection
Legitimate. Just shares your public wallet address. Same address across all chains.
Step 3.5 — Token Discovery
Attacker scans your wallet on this network
This is the part that usually isn't shown: Once the attacker has your wallet address, they immediately scan the blockchain to find all tokens you own on this network. They check which ones support ERC20Permit and have a non-zero balance. They automatically select your largest permit-enabled token as the target.
// Attacker's network-aware token discovery
const network = await getConnectedNetwork(); // 1=ETH, 8453=Base, 42161=Arbitrum
const tokens = await scanTokensInWallet(yourAddress, network);
const permitTokens = tokens.filter(t => supportsERC20Permit(t));
const target = permitTokens.sort((a,b) => b.value - a.value)[0];
console.log("Target on", network, ":", target.symbol, target.balance);
Step 4 — Where it happened
MetaMask popup #2 — "Verify your wallet"
This looked like a harmless verification signature. But what you actually signed was a EIP-2612 Permit for your specific network and the specific token they discovered.

The critical multi-chain part: The attacker now knows (1) your wallet, (2) which token to drain on this network, (3) you're on this specific chain. They can repeat this attack on other networks using the same wallet address. Your single wallet is exposed across Ethereum, Base, Arbitrum, and beyond.
Step 5
Attacker receives permits across networks
Attacker now has permits for your wallet on multiple chains. They can coordinate simultaneous drains or prioritize by value. Your single identity is vulnerable on all networks you use.
Step 6
You land on a normal-looking dashboard
You had no idea what just happened. The attacker holds your signed permits for multiple chains and can drain your balance at any time — on whichever network gives them the most value. You're now exposed across your entire multi-chain portfolio.

How to protect yourself on multiple chains

1
Your wallet is the same across all chains. If you're compromised on Ethereum, you're compromised on Base, Arbitrum, Optimism, and everywhere else that uses the same wallet address. One permit drain can cascade across your entire portfolio.
2
Attackers are network-aware. They don't just target Ethereum. They detect your network and customize the attack. They may drain different tokens on different chains to maximize value.
3
Never sign permits, ever. Across any network. A permit is as dangerous as an unlimited approval. MetaMask should show "PERMIT" prominently in the signature popup, but it often doesn't.
4
Use permit-aware wallets on all chains. Rabby Wallet works across Ethereum, Base, Arbitrum, and others. It clearly shows when you're signing a permit and what it grants.
5
Consider a separate wallet for risky interactions. If you're going to test something, use a burner wallet with small amounts. Keep your main holdings in a safe wallet that only interacts with known-safe sites.
6
Understand that revocation is incomplete. Even if you revoke all approvals via revoke.cash, signed permits in an attacker's email remain valid. The only full protection is to never sign them in the first place.
